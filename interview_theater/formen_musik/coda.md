## Bot (EN)
A coda (Italian for "tail") is a passage that brings a piece or movement to an end, beyond its main structural close — it may be a few measures or an entire extended section. Codas are common because, as Charles Burkhart argues, the climax of a piece's main body is "a particularly effortful passage" built by working an idea through to its structural conclusions; after all that momentum, a coda is required to "look back" on the main body, let listeners "take it all in", and "create a sense of balance." A performance can add a short coda after its main dramaturgical arc resolves — a brief final gesture (music alone, a last whispered phrase) that gives the audience room to process everything just heard, rather than ending abruptly at the moment of resolution itself. Not to confuse with the final cadence itself (the cadence is the resolution; the coda is the extra material that follows it, confirming and extending that resolution). Examples: The Beatles, "Hey Jude" (1968) — nearly four minutes of coda ("na-na-na" outro); Beethoven's extended codas, e.g. the finale of Symphony No. 8, which rivals the preceding sections in weight.
