# Form: Dialogue — spoken scene (script by the Herkules measure)

A spoken scene, several characters. What you write is a completely ordinary
script for spoken theatre: lines, stage directions, scenes with a beginning
and an end. **No choreography.**

**Why this is so.** The script is source material for the team's work, not
its score. The director and the choreographer work with it in rehearsal:
they may cut the text, rearrange it, throw it out completely, take it only
as a starting point and develop movement, music and stage themselves. That
is why you give them situations, conflicts and rhythm -- and take nothing
away from them in advance. A script that already writes the staging along
with it is useless, because it blocks the actual work.

The numbers below were measured on a finished, performed script of the same
kind (Herkules.exe: nine scenes, around 12,300 words). They are target
values, not a counting exercise: you hit them by writing this way, not by
doing the maths.

## The rules

1. **Write a spoken scene.** Lines and stage directions, nothing else.
   No movement blocks, no dance sections, no separate level for the
   body.
2. **A scene has 700 to 1,500 words.** That is the measured range
   (median 1,400); eight to twelve minutes of playing time. Shorter is
   allowed, longer is not.
3. **Four fifths of the text are spoken, at most one fifth is
   direction.** Target value: 80 % spoken text, at most 20 % stage direction.
   The model script had 35 % direction -- decided on 05.09.2026: only the
   essentials of that stay. A stage direction is only in if the scene can't be
   understood without it: entrance, exit, an action that turns the
   conflict, an object that is needed later. Everything else
   -- looks, postures, pace, mood, movement in the space -- belongs to
   rehearsal and is NOT in the text.
4. **For every stage direction there are at least five lines.** Direction
   goes where something happens that you otherwise wouldn't see -- not next
   to every sentence, not as a rhythm-maker between the lines. A page of
   dialogue without a single bracket is normal.
5. **A line has eight words and two sentences on average.** That is the
   normal case, not the upper limit: every tenth line may go over forty
   words when a character really launches into something. None goes over
   120 words.
6. **Roughly every third line is a question.** Measured 32 %. Questions
   drive the scene; statements stop it. Not every question gets an
   answer.
7. **Every tenth line is a single word.** "Yes." "When?" "Nothing." That
   is rhythm, not a mannerism -- three of them in a row are two too many.
8. **How many characters are in a scene is set by the planning (the
   field "Who") -- not by this rule block.** A scene with one character is
   just as valid as one with all of them. Whoever isn't in the details of
   this scene isn't there.
9. **Conflict is played, not named.** When a character says "I feel like a
   stranger here", the scene is already over. Show it: she gets the name of
   the street wrong, she doesn't take her coat off, she answers the wrong
   question. A sentence about a feeling is allowed if the scene can't show
   it -- it almost always can.
10. **The conflict is physical, not argumentative.** Someone walks away,
    someone holds someone back, someone stands up, someone stays seated.
    Write situations in which bodies rub against each other -- someone else
    can make movement out of that. Don't name that movement.
11. **Give the conflict an object on which it is decided.** A
    phone, a door, a jacket, a note. The decision is an
    action on this thing, not a sentence.
12. **No character explains their motivation in complete sentences.**
    People rarely know why they do what they do, and say it even more
    rarely. Nobody says "I'm doing this because I'm afraid that ...".
13. **No character states information that both already know.** "As you
    know, we've been married for twelve years" only exists in theatre that
    is bad.
14. **Characters sound different.** Different sentence length,
    different register, different filler words. The speech profile and the
    quotes above are there for that: whoever speaks short and clipped there
    speaks that way here too. If you could strike out the names and no
    longer know who is speaking, something is wrong -- and so fundamentally
    that the text needs to be rewritten.
15. **Pauses, interruptions and the unsaid are tools, not
    embarrassment.** A character may break off mid-sentence, a question may
    stay unanswered, two characters may talk past each other. A scene in
    which every question gets an answer is an interview, not a
    dialogue.
16. **No monologue longer than six lines.** If a character has to say more,
    interrupt them -- through the other character, through an action,
    through a noise. If a character really needs to launch into something,
    that is a scene of its own in the Monologue form.
17. **Repeat word for word, don't vary.** A recurring event appears letter
    for letter the same the second and third time; only the waiting time in
    between grows.
18. **The rhythm is in the language, not in a count.**
    Lists, interruptions, a sentence that comes back, an alternation of
    long and short lines. That is your contribution to the staging -- a
    choreographer hears it.
19. **Set clear turning points.** Two to three places per scene where
    something tips, and you can see on which action it tips. A script
    without turning points can't be translated into movement.
20. **Write a character's most important moment without text.** An action,
    a silence that the others have to fill.
21. **Refuse the victory and the summary.** No closing sentence that
    explains the scene; no reconciliation paragraph after the argument. The
    admission stays, the reconciliation is cut. The last line
    is the second-to-last plus a twist, not a conclusion -- better to stop
    too early than too late.
22. **At the end something is different from the beginning** -- and you see
    it in an action, not in a sentence about it.

## Output form

As in the model script: scene heading with duration, below it one or two
paragraphs of place and opening image, then lines. Names in capitals.
A stage direction either as its own paragraph in brackets or directly after
the colon in brackets -- sparingly: at most every tenth line carries
an inline direction, and only if it names an action, not a mood.

```
SCENE <N>: <TITLE IN CAPITALS> approx. <X> min
<Place, time of day, what can be seen. One or two paragraphs.>

<CHARACTER A>:(doesn't look up)Line.
<CHARACTER B>:Line.
(<CHARACTER C> comes in. Sits down at the end of the bench.)
<CHARACTER C>:Line.
<CHARACTER A>:Line.
(Pause. Nobody says anything.)
```

The header lines `TITEL:` and `KURZ:` from the basic form still come first,
at the very top, before the scene heading.

## What you don't write

- **Choreography.** No step sequences, no arm, foot or
  weight instructions, no formations, no paths through the space as a rule.
- **Counts and musical bars.** No eights, no counts, no BPM,
  no "for four bars".
- **Dance vocabulary.** Popping, locking, house, krump, breaking, waacking,
  cypher, unison, canon, freeze, tableau -- none of it belongs in this
  text. That is the language of rehearsal, not of the script.
- **Separate movement levels.** No `[MOVEMENT]`, `[DANCE]` or
  `[TABLEAU]` blocks, no second column, no markers other than brackets.
- **Music, light and video instructions.** No `>> Music:`, no
  beat cues, no projections.
- **Set and props** beyond what people carry on their
  bodies. No furniture that first has to be built.
- **Mood directions.** `(sad)`, `(full of rage)`, `(thoughtful)`. What you
  can't do isn't written.
- **Narrative prose.** No paragraph describing what a character feels or
  what the image means.
- **The explaining sentence.** Every line that names the theme of the scene
  instead of playing it is cut. If something is missing afterwards, it is
  rightly missing.
