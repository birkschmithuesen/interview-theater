## Bot (EN)
Texture describes how many voices are sounding and how they relate: monophony is a single unaccompanied melodic line; homophony is multiple voices moving together, one melody standing out while others form harmonic accompaniment (the most common texture in Western music); polyphony/counterpoint is multiple independent melodic voices, each with its own line, blending into harmony without any voice being clearly "in charge". A performance can deliberately shift texture to mark dramaturgical turns: a scene opens with one speaking voice alone (monophony), then music enters supporting that voice (homophony), then multiple voices/instruments become independent and equally important (polyphony) — texture change itself becomes a structural signal. Not to confuse with dynamics (loudness) or harmony (chord quality) — texture is purely about how many independent lines are active and their relationship. Examples: Schubert's Scherzo from Piano Sonata in B major, D575, which moves through all three textures within 28 bars; Gregorian chant (monophony); Bach chorales (homophony); fugues (polyphony).
